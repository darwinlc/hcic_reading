Gear Fundamentals

A gear is a circular mechanical part with teeth around its edge. When two gears mesh (touch) together, they transfer force and motion from one shaft to another.

Gear Ratio Calculations

The gear ratio tells you how the speed and torque change between the input and output. This is the most important formula in this chapter!

$$i = \frac{n_1}{n_2} = \frac{z_2}{z_1} = \frac{d_2}{d_1}$$
igear ratio (transmission ratio)
n₁drive gear speed (RPM) — INPUT
n₂driven gear speed (RPM) — OUTPUT
z₁teeth on drive gear
z₂teeth on driven gear

🌟 The Golden Rule — Easy to Remember

Gear ratio  =  Driven gear teeth  ÷  Drive gear teeth
              =       z₂           ÷       z₁

Driven > Drive  →  i > 1  →  SLOW DOWN  (gain torque 💪)
Driven < Drive  →  i < 1  →  SPEED UP   (lose torque)
Driven = Drive  →  i = 1  →  Same speed (direction reverses)
✏️ Worked Example 1
Problem: Drive gear has $z_1 = 20$ teeth. Driven gear has $z_2 = 60$ teeth. Drive gear spins at $n_1 = 300$ RPM. Find the driven gear speed $n_2$.

Step-by-step solution:

Step 1 — Calculate the gear ratio:

$$i = \frac{z_2}{z_1} = \frac{60}{20} = 3$$

Step 2 — Find output speed:

$$n_2 = \frac{n_1}{i} = \frac{300}{3} = 100 \text{ RPM}$$
✅ Output speed = 100 RPM — slowed down by a factor of 3

Multi-Stage Gear Trains

When one gear pair can't achieve a large enough speed reduction, you chain multiple stages in series. The total ratio is simply all stages multiplied together.

Input
→

i₂

⚙️ Stage 3
i₃
→
🎯 Output
Result
$$i_{\text{total}} = i_1 \times i_2 \times i_3 \times \cdots$$
Multiply all individual stage ratios together to get the overall ratio
✏️ Worked Example 2
Problem: A 3-stage gear train. Motor speed = 900 RPM. Find the final output speed.

· Stage 1: $z_1=10,\ z_2=30\ \Rightarrow\ i_1 = 3$
· Stage 2: $z_3=15,\ z_4=45\ \Rightarrow\ i_2 = 3$
· Stage 3: $z_5=12,\ z_6=60\ \Rightarrow\ i_3 = 5$

Step 1 — Total gear ratio:

$$i_{\text{total}} = 3 \times 3 \times 5 = 45$$

Step 2 — Output speed:

$$n_{\text{output}} = \frac{900}{45} = 20 \text{ RPM}$$
✅ Final output = 20 RPM — reduced by a factor of 45! 🚀

Torque & Speed Relationship

Here's a key insight: power is conserved (ignoring friction). This means slowing down always increases torque — you trade speed for strength!

$$P = T \times \omega = \text{constant}$$
Ppower (Watts) — stays the same
Ttorque (N·m)
ωangular velocity (rad/s)

The Speed ↔ Torque Seesaw

You can't have more speed AND more torque at the same time!
🐇
Speed
↓ decreases
when you slow down
⇌
💪
Torque
↑ increases
when you slow down
$$\frac{T_2}{T_1} = \frac{n_1}{n_2} = i$$
✏️ Worked Example 3
Problem: Motor outputs 1000 RPM at 0.5 N·m torque. It passes through a gearbox with ratio $i = 10$. Find output speed and torque (assume 100% efficiency).
$$n_{\text{output}} = \frac{1000}{10} = 100 \text{ RPM}$$ $$T_{\text{output}} = 0.5 \times 10 = 5 \text{ N·m}$$
✅ Output: 100 RPM and 5 N·m — 10× more torque!

Speed-Up vs Slow-Down at a Glance

Driven teeth  >  Drive teeth  →  Slow down, gain torque  ✅  (common in robots)
Driven teeth  <  Drive teeth  →  Speed up, lose torque
Driven teeth  =  Drive teeth  →  Same speed (direction reverses only)
🔑
Key insight: Most robot actuators use reduction gears (slow down) because motors spin fast but weakly. Gears trade speed for the extra torque needed to lift, push, or grip.
